Custom Isolator Design for Vibration and Seismic Control

anitaa September 28, 2026

Equipment isolation is rarely a matter of selecting a component based on weight alone. In commercial buildings, healthcare facilities, industrial plants, data centers, marine applications, and other technically demanding environments, the isolation system must respond to the actual behavior of the equipment and the conditions surrounding it. That is where custom isolator design becomes an engineering discipline rather than a simple product-selection exercise.

A properly engineered isolation system considers operating weight, center of gravity, mounting locations, excitation frequency, static deflection, stiffness, damping, expected movement, environmental exposure, and the way loads transfer into the supporting structure. For rotating equipment, operating speed and excitation characteristics can be especially important because an isolator that appears adequate under static loading may perform poorly when subjected to dynamic forces.

Seismic requirements introduce another layer of complexity. Vibration isolation is intended to reduce the transmission of mechanical energy, while seismic restraint limits potentially damaging movement during an earthquake. These objectives can interact, but they are not interchangeable. A successful design may therefore require an isolation element, restraint mechanism, structural attachment, and anchorage system that function together without compromising the required vibration performance.

For U.S. construction projects, the engineering process may also involve the IBC, ASCE 7, CBC, ACI 318, AISC standards, HCAI requirements for applicable healthcare projects, equipment manufacturer requirements, project specifications, and the requirements of the Engineer of Record and Authority Having Jurisdiction.

The objective is not to make every project custom. Standard products remain appropriate for many applications. Customization becomes valuable when equipment characteristics, performance requirements, geometry, environmental conditions, or seismic and structural interfaces cannot be adequately addressed by a standard configuration. A disciplined custom vibration isolator design process identifies those requirements first and develops the isolation solution around them.

What Is Custom Isolator Design?

Custom isolator design is the engineering process of developing an isolation component or complete isolation assembly for a particular equipment application. Instead of beginning with an existing catalog model and attempting to fit the equipment to it, the design begins with measurable requirements and determines what the isolator must accomplish.

A typical evaluation starts with equipment weight and load distribution, but those are only the first inputs. Engineers may need the equipment's center of gravity, mounting-point locations, operating speed, excitation frequency, expected dynamic forces, equipment base configuration, allowable displacement, environmental conditions, and available structural attachment points. The required static deflection and target dynamic characteristics can then be established from the application's objectives.

The distinction between a customized configuration and a genuinely engineered solution is important. A manufacturer may modify a mounting plate or provide a different dimension without changing the fundamental isolation behavior. A custom vibration isolator design, by contrast, may require changes to spring characteristics, elastomer geometry, wire-rope configuration, damping behavior, load distribution, restraint geometry, or the complete support assembly.

The engineering objective is to establish a relationship between the equipment and its supporting structure:

Equipment → operating load → excitation → isolator properties → vibration response → support reaction

For applications involving earthquakes, the relationship expands:

Equipment → isolator → restraint → allowable movement → structural attachment → anchorage

This approach is useful for HVAC equipment, rotating machinery, industrial machinery, generators, marine engines, compressors, pumps, and specialized mechanical systems. It is also relevant when equipment must fit within a restricted mounting envelope or when an existing structural condition makes standard hardware impractical.

Custom design should not automatically be treated as better than a standard isolator. The purpose is to achieve an appropriate, verifiable engineering result when the application's requirements justify customization.

How Are Custom Vibration Isolators Engineered?

The engineering process begins with application data rather than with a particular isolator technology. For a mechanical system, engineers first establish what the equipment weighs, how that weight is distributed, where it is supported, and how it behaves while operating. A machine that weighs the same as another machine may require a completely different isolation solution if its operating speed, excitation, mounting geometry, or dynamic characteristics differ.

From Equipment Data to Isolation Requirements

Important inputs can include operating and shutdown conditions, static and dynamic loads, center of gravity, mounting-point locations, operating speed, excitation frequencies, equipment base stiffness, environmental exposure, and installation constraints. For rotating machinery, the relationship between operating speed and excitation frequency is particularly important.

Once the operating requirements are understood, the design can establish appropriate isolator properties. These may include load capacity, static stiffness, dynamic stiffness, static deflection, natural frequency, damping characteristics, allowable travel, and mounting geometry.

Load, Frequency, Stiffness, and Damping

An isolator functions within a dynamic system. Its performance depends on the relationship between the supported mass and the isolator's stiffness and damping characteristics. The engineering question is therefore not simply whether an isolator can carry the equipment but whether it can carry the equipment while producing the required dynamic response.

Uneven loading also matters. Multiple isolators supporting one equipment base may experience different reactions because of the center of gravity or asymmetric equipment geometry. The design must account for those differences rather than assuming that total equipment weight can simply be divided equally among mounting points.

This is why vibration isolator engineering often requires application-specific calculations. The final configuration should be coordinated with the equipment base, structural support, and installation conditions so that the isolation system performs as designed after installation, not merely on paper.

How Do Static Deflection and Natural Frequency Affect Isolator Performance?

Static deflection is one of the fundamental parameters in vibration isolation because it is related to the stiffness of the support system and, for many spring-based systems, to its natural frequency. A simplified single-degree-of-freedom model illustrates the relationship between supported mass, stiffness, and natural frequency. In practice, however, real equipment systems can involve multiple degrees of freedom, nonlinear behavior, damping, flexible bases, and frequency-dependent material properties.

For a spring-supported system, increasing the supported static deflection generally corresponds to reducing vertical natural frequency. A lower natural frequency can create a larger frequency separation between the isolator system and the equipment's operating excitation, which can improve isolation when the system operates sufficiently above resonance.

Static Versus Dynamic Stiffness

Static stiffness describes the response under slowly applied loading. Dynamic stiffness describes behavior under oscillating or time-varying loading and may differ because of material properties, frequency, amplitude, preload, and construction. Elastomeric components can be particularly sensitive to these factors.

For this reason, specifying only a nominal spring rate or static load capacity may not fully describe an isolator's operating performance. Engineers may need dynamic characteristics and test data relevant to the intended application.

Avoiding Resonance

The ratio between excitation frequency and system natural frequency strongly influences transmissibility. Near resonance, vibration can be amplified rather than isolated. Damping can reduce the magnitude of resonance, although excessive damping can also affect high-frequency isolation behavior.

A custom isolator design therefore needs to consider the entire operating frequency range rather than one isolated operating point. Variable-speed drives, startup and shutdown conditions, harmonics, and changing process loads can all affect the response.

The practical objective is to establish an isolation system whose natural frequency, damping, load capacity, and movement characteristics are compatible with the equipment's operating environment.

Which Type of Custom Isolator Is Right for the Application?

There is no single isolation technology that is appropriate for every application. Spring, wire rope, rubber/metal, and elastomeric systems have different mechanical characteristics and should be selected according to the performance requirements.

Custom Spring Isolators

Spring isolators can provide relatively low natural frequencies and substantial vertical deflection, making them useful for many mechanical and HVAC applications. A custom spring isolator design can address unusual load ranges, mounting dimensions, spring characteristics, or restraint requirements.

Wire Rope Isolators

Wire rope isolators use formed wire-rope elements to provide resilient support. Their characteristics can make them useful where shock, vibration, multidirectional loading, corrosion resistance, compact geometry, or durability under demanding conditions are important. A custom wire rope isolator design can be configured around specific mounting and load requirements.

Rubber and Elastomeric Isolators

Rubber/metal and elastomeric systems can provide useful damping and compact mounting arrangements. Different compounds and geometries can produce different stiffness and environmental characteristics. Neoprene, EPDM, natural rubber compounds, and synthetic elastomers may be considered according to temperature, chemical exposure, weathering, fatigue, and dynamic requirements.

Selection should therefore consider load capacity, stiffness, damping, frequency response, shock, geometry, environmental exposure, maintenance, and expected service life. Acoustic hangers and floor isolation systems address different installation arrangements but follow the same underlying principle: the isolation system must be matched to the mechanical behavior and installation conditions.

When Should Equipment Use Restrained or Captive Vibration Isolators?

Vibration isolation permits controlled relative movement between equipment and its support. Some installations, however, cannot allow unrestricted movement. Equipment located in seismic regions, elevated structures, mechanical rooms, rooftops, or other exposed environments may require a restraint strategy that limits movement while preserving the intended isolation function.

Restrained and captive configurations can provide controlled travel through mechanical restraints, housings, stops, or other geometries. A restrained vibration isolator may allow normal operating movement but engage or limit movement beyond a defined range. A captive vibration isolator can similarly maintain the equipment and isolation assembly within a controlled physical envelope.

The restraint must be designed as part of the system. If a restraint is too stiff, incorrectly positioned, or permanently engaged during normal operation, it can create an unintended vibration path and reduce isolation effectiveness. If excessive clearance exists, equipment movement during a seismic event may be greater than intended.

Important design variables include isolation gap, allowable travel, restraint direction, load path, mounting geometry, structural attachment, installation tolerance, and expected seismic movement. The restraint also needs to transfer its design forces into the supporting structure through an appropriate connection and anchorage system.

This is particularly important for mechanical equipment that must remain operational or remain attached during an earthquake. A vibration isolator by itself should not be assumed to provide seismic protection.

The correct design balances two potentially competing objectives: allow enough movement for effective vibration isolation while controlling movement when restraint is required. That balance is one of the primary reasons application-specific engineering can be necessary.

How Does Custom Isolator Design Address Seismic Requirements?

Seismic requirements should be considered separately from vibration-performance requirements and then integrated into the overall support strategy. Building codes and seismic standards address earthquake effects, but they do not automatically define every dynamic-performance characteristic needed for an equipment isolation system.

Vibration Isolation Versus Seismic Restraint

Vibration isolation reduces the transmission of mechanical vibration between equipment and a supporting structure. Seismic restraint controls equipment movement and transfers earthquake-induced forces into the building. An isolation assembly may require both functions, but each must be evaluated for its own purpose.

For applicable projects, ASCE 7 and the IBC provide important seismic design requirements, while the CBC governs applicable California construction. Project specifications, equipment requirements, and AHJ requirements can impose additional criteria.

Equipment Anchorage and Structural Load Paths

The seismic load path should be understandable:

Equipment → isolator → restraint → support → connection → anchor → structure

That path may involve structural steel, concrete foundations, equipment frames, housekeeping pads, anchor bolts, concrete anchors, or post-installed anchors. The capacity of the isolator does not establish the capacity of the entire load path.

For reinforced-concrete interfaces, ACI 318 provisions may become relevant to anchorage design. Structural steel interfaces may require consideration of applicable AISC provisions. Proprietary anchors may also require appropriate evaluation documentation.

Healthcare facilities introduce additional coordination requirements. Projects under HCAI jurisdiction may have requirements beyond those found in a conventional commercial installation, making early coordination among the equipment manufacturer, isolation designer, structural engineer, and project team particularly important.

How Are Custom Isolators Designed for HVAC and Mechanical Equipment?

HVAC equipment frequently creates vibration-control challenges because fans, pumps, compressors, motors, and other rotating components generate dynamic forces that can travel through equipment supports into the building. The problem can become more significant when equipment is installed above occupied spaces, near sensitive areas, or on rooftops.

For an air-handling unit, for example, the design may need to account for operating weight, fan speed, motor arrangement, center of gravity, support points, casing configuration, and available clearance. A chiller or pump may introduce different loading and excitation characteristics. Cooling towers can combine rotating equipment vibration with substantial structural and environmental considerations.

Vibration isolation for HVAC equipment should therefore be integrated with the complete mechanical support arrangement. Isolators may be mounted beneath equipment, attached to structural frames, integrated with inertia bases, or incorporated into suspended systems.

Seismic restraint can introduce another design layer. A vibration-isolated air-handling unit may need controlled seismic movement while maintaining adequate isolation during normal operation. Restraint cables, captive configurations, seismic snubbers, or other restraint strategies may be appropriate depending on the project requirements.

Flexible connections also matter. Ductwork, piping, conduit, and other connected systems can unintentionally bypass an isolation system if they are not coordinated for movement. The isolation assembly should therefore be considered alongside MEP routing, connection flexibility, structural support, access, and maintenance clearances.

For commercial buildings, hospitals, laboratories, data centers, and other sensitive facilities, the engineering process should begin before fabrication. Early coordination can prevent conflicts between equipment dimensions, isolator locations, structural framing, seismic restraints, and adjacent MEP systems.

How Does Custom Isolation Work for Industrial and Rotating Machinery?

Industrial machinery can impose more demanding dynamic requirements than conventional building equipment. Motors, pumps, compressors, generators, manufacturing equipment, process machinery, and marine propulsion systems can operate continuously at significant speeds and generate forces that vary with operating condition.

Operating Speed and Excitation Frequency

Machine speed can be converted into a fundamental rotational frequency, but the excitation environment may also contain harmonics and other frequencies. The isolator's natural frequency must therefore be evaluated relative to the relevant operating spectrum.

A custom isolation system can be particularly useful when a machine has a narrow operating envelope, variable-speed operation, unusual excitation, or mounting constraints that make standard equipment mounts unsuitable.

Shock, Impact, and High-Cycle Loading

Some industrial and marine applications also involve shock, impact, high-cycle loading, or multidirectional motion. Wire rope and specialized elastomeric systems can be considered where their characteristics align with the application. Material fatigue, corrosion, temperature, and service environment become important design inputs.

Marine engine isolation is an example where the isolation system may need to accommodate a combination of vibration, shock, structural movement, and harsh environmental exposure. Stainless steel components, corrosion-resistant finishes, appropriate elastomer compounds, and engineered mounting geometry may all become relevant.

The design should also consider equipment-base stiffness. An isolator may have appropriate characteristics in isolation but still produce undesirable behavior if the equipment support structure is excessively flexible. Load distribution among mounting points can affect both isolation performance and equipment alignment.

For high-cycle machinery, durability cannot be separated from dynamic performance. The design should consider expected cycles, operating conditions, material fatigue, attachment details, and inspection or maintenance requirements. This is where custom industrial vibration isolators become an engineering solution rather than simply a modified mounting component.

What Materials Are Used in Custom Isolator Systems?

Material selection for an isolation system should follow the required mechanical and environmental performance. The same material may behave differently depending on geometry, preload, temperature, frequency, manufacturing process, and service environment.

Metals and Structural Components

Carbon steel and structural steel are commonly used for mounting plates, housings, brackets, equipment frames, and support assemblies where their strength and fabrication characteristics suit the application. Stainless steel may be appropriate where corrosion resistance is important, including certain marine, industrial, and exposed environments.

Spring steel is used where resilient spring behavior is required, while wire rope can form the active resilient element in wire-rope isolators. Aluminum may be appropriate in selected applications where reduced weight and corrosion considerations justify its use, provided the structural and fatigue requirements are satisfied.

Protective treatments such as galvanizing or powder coating can address environmental exposure for suitable components, but coatings should not be treated as substitutes for appropriate material selection.

Elastomers and Resilient Materials

Natural rubber compounds, neoprene, EPDM, and other synthetic elastomers can provide different combinations of stiffness, damping, environmental resistance, and temperature performance. Rubber/metal composites can combine an elastomeric element with a structural metal interface.

The geometry of an elastomer can be just as important as its nominal material specification. Thickness, bonded area, shape factor, preload, and deformation mode can influence effective stiffness and damping.

For all materials, the design should consider fatigue performance, temperature range, corrosion, chemical exposure, moisture, ultraviolet exposure where applicable, service life, and maintenance conditions. A technically appropriate custom isolator engineering process connects these factors to the actual operating environment instead of treating material choice as a simple catalog decision.

How Are Custom Isolators Integrated With Structural Supports?

An isolator is only one component in the complete equipment support system. Its performance depends on the interface between the equipment, isolator, support frame, structural member, and anchorage.

A typical load path may begin at an equipment mounting point and pass through a base plate or isolator assembly into a structural steel frame, concrete housekeeping pad, inertia base, or building structure. Each interface introduces potential issues involving strength, stiffness, geometry, tolerances, and installation.

Custom mounting plates can be useful when equipment mounting points do not align with available structural supports. Equipment support frames can also distribute reactions into multiple structural members. Inertia bases may be incorporated where additional mass or stiffness is part of the vibration-control strategy.

Anchorage requires the same system-level perspective. Anchor bolts, concrete anchors, post-installed anchors, and structural steel attachments must be selected and designed for the applicable load conditions. A high-capacity isolator cannot compensate for an inadequate anchor or weak structural connection.

BIM and 3D CAD coordination can make these relationships easier to evaluate. Equipment dimensions, mounting points, isolator locations, support steel, MEP routing, structural framing, and access clearances can be represented before fabrication begins.

For complex projects, the result should be more than an isolator drawing. The deliverable may include engineering calculations, coordinated CAD models, fabrication drawings, mounting details, restraint geometry, and documentation supporting installation.

This integrated approach is especially valuable when the isolation system must interact with seismic bracing, structural steel, concrete construction, or crowded mechanical spaces. The engineering objective is to create a continuous and verifiable load path while preserving the required isolation behavior.

How Do BIM, CAD, and Custom Fabrication Support Isolator Design?

Custom isolation becomes significantly more practical when engineering, digital coordination, and fabrication are treated as connected stages of one workflow. BIM 3D CAD modeling can establish the physical relationship between equipment, isolators, structural supports, MEP systems, and surrounding construction before components reach the fabrication floor.

A coordinated model can identify issues such as insufficient clearance, conflicting anchor locations, inaccessible fasteners, equipment-frame interference, or incorrect mounting-point assumptions. For restrained systems, it can also help visualize travel limits and the relationship between the isolator and restraint hardware.

Once the engineering configuration is established, fabrication drawings can translate the design into manufacturable components. Depending on the application, custom assemblies may involve laser cutting, plasma cutting, welding, forming, machining, stamping, galvanizing, or powder coating.

Custom metal fabrication can support mounting plates, brackets, equipment frames, spring housings, support assemblies, and other structural components required to integrate the isolation system with the equipment.

The important distinction is that fabrication follows the engineering requirements. Producing a custom-shaped steel component does not, by itself, constitute custom isolator design. The isolation behavior, load capacity, stiffness, damping, restraint requirements, and structural interfaces must first be established.

The same principle applies to installation. Fabrication tolerances, field dimensions, anchor locations, equipment alignment, and access requirements can influence whether the finished assembly performs as intended.

For technically complex projects, the complete workflow can therefore be represented as:

Requirements → analysis → isolator design → seismic evaluation → structural coordination → BIM/CAD → fabrication → installation → verification

That continuity reduces the risk of treating engineering, fabrication, and field installation as disconnected activities.

What Codes and Project Requirements Affect Custom Isolator Design?

Custom isolation projects in the United States may be governed by several overlapping sources of requirements. The exact criteria depend on building type, jurisdiction, equipment function, structural system, seismic conditions, project specifications, and the role of the isolation assembly.

The International Building Code (IBC) establishes a broad framework for building construction, including structural and seismic provisions. ASCE 7 provides seismic design criteria used within that framework, including requirements relevant to applicable nonstructural components and equipment. In California, the California Building Code (CBC) establishes applicable state requirements.

These standards should not be presented as complete vibration-isolator performance specifications. Vibration requirements can also come from equipment manufacturers, project specifications, acoustic criteria, owner requirements, testing procedures, and application-specific engineering analysis.

For healthcare facilities, HCAI requirements can become particularly important. The term OSHPD remains relevant in discussing California's historical healthcare regulatory framework and pre-approval terminology, while current project requirements should be evaluated under the applicable HCAI framework.

Structural interfaces may introduce additional standards. ACI 318 can be relevant to reinforced-concrete anchorage, while AISC 360 may apply to structural steel components. Where seismic structural systems fall within applicable seismic provisions, additional AISC requirements may need consideration. Proprietary anchors and fastening systems may also require appropriate ICC evaluation documentation.

The Engineer of Record, equipment manufacturer, project specifications, and Authority Having Jurisdiction can establish requirements that are not captured by a single standard.

For this reason, code compliance should be treated as part of the engineering process rather than as a label attached to an isolator after design. The appropriate approach is to identify the governing requirements, determine which apply to the equipment and support system, document the design assumptions, and coordinate the resulting isolation and restraint assembly with the project team.

Frequently Asked Questions About Custom Isolator Design

What is custom isolator design?

Custom isolator design is the engineering process of developing an isolation component or assembly around the characteristics of a specific application. It can involve load capacity, static deflection, stiffness, damping, natural frequency, excitation frequency, geometry, environmental conditions, seismic movement, and structural attachment. It differs from simply selecting an existing catalog isolator because the engineering requirements determine the configuration.

When should I use a custom vibration isolator instead of a standard isolator?

A custom solution may be appropriate when standard products cannot satisfy the required load range, mounting geometry, natural frequency, dynamic characteristics, environmental exposure, allowable movement, or restraint configuration. Standard products should remain the starting point when they adequately satisfy the application. Customization is justified by measurable requirements rather than by the assumption that custom components inherently perform better.

How does equipment weight affect isolator selection?

Equipment weight determines the static load that the isolation system must support, but it is only one design input. Engineers may also need to consider load distribution, center of gravity, mounting-point locations, operating speed, excitation forces, equipment-base stiffness, and dynamic response. Two machines with identical weights can require different isolator configurations because their operating characteristics are different.

What is the relationship between static deflection and natural frequency?

For many spring-based isolation systems, greater static deflection corresponds generally to lower natural frequency. The natural frequency is important because isolation performance depends on the relationship between excitation frequency and the isolation system's natural frequency. Actual behavior also depends on damping, dynamic stiffness, supported mass, and the complete mechanical system, so static deflection alone should not be treated as a complete performance specification.

How do spring, wire rope, and elastomeric isolators differ?

Spring isolators are commonly associated with substantial deflection and low natural frequencies. Wire rope isolators can be useful for applications involving shock, vibration, multidirectional loading, and demanding environments. Elastomeric and rubber/metal isolators can provide compact configurations and useful damping, with performance influenced by compound and geometry. Selection should be based on load, frequency, damping, environment, geometry, fatigue, movement, and application requirements.

Can a vibration isolator also provide seismic protection?

A vibration isolator should not automatically be considered a seismic restraint. Vibration isolation and seismic restraint have different objectives. An integrated system can combine isolation with restraints, captive configurations, snubbers, cables, or other movement-control mechanisms when required. The complete load path from equipment through the restraint and structural attachment to the building must be evaluated for applicable seismic requirements.

What information is needed to design a custom equipment isolator?

Useful information can include equipment weight, center of gravity, dimensions, mounting-point locations, operating speed, excitation frequencies, expected dynamic loads, equipment-base characteristics, allowable movement, operating temperature, environmental exposure, available structural attachment points, and applicable project specifications. Manufacturer data and installation constraints are also important.

Can custom isolators be designed for HVAC equipment?

Yes. HVAC applications can include air-handling units, chillers, pumps, fans, compressors, cooling towers, boilers, and rooftop equipment. The design may address operating vibration, equipment loading, support reactions, structural interfaces, seismic restraint, flexible connections, and installation clearances. For healthcare and other sensitive facilities, project-specific requirements can make early coordination particularly important.

Are custom isolators suitable for rotating industrial machinery?

They can be, provided the isolation system is engineered around the machine's operating conditions. Rotating machinery can generate forces associated with rotational speed, harmonics, imbalance, startup, shutdown, and changing process conditions. The design may therefore require analysis of excitation frequency, natural frequency, damping, dynamic stiffness, equipment-base behavior, fatigue, and mounting-point reactions.

How are custom isolators connected to structural supports?

Connections can involve base plates, mounting plates, brackets, equipment frames, inertia bases, structural steel, concrete foundations, anchor bolts, concrete anchors, or post-installed anchors. The complete load path must be evaluated rather than relying on the capacity of the isolator alone. Structural attachment should also account for installation tolerances, access, clearance, and applicable seismic requirements.

What materials are used in custom vibration isolators?

Materials can include carbon steel, stainless steel, structural steel, spring steel, wire rope, elastomers, natural rubber compounds, neoprene, EPDM, synthetic rubber, and rubber/metal composites. Material selection depends on required stiffness, damping, load capacity, fatigue, temperature, corrosion resistance, chemical exposure, environmental conditions, and service life.

Can custom isolator design include BIM, fabrication, and seismic engineering?

Yes. For complex projects, integrating these disciplines can help maintain continuity from analysis to installation. BIM and 3D CAD can coordinate equipment and support geometry; structural and seismic engineering can address load paths and restraint; and custom fabrication can produce the physical mounting plates, brackets, frames, or other components required by the engineered configuration. The resulting workflow connects design requirements with the actual construction conditions.

Conclusion: Engineering Custom Isolation Around the Application

Effective isolation begins with understanding the equipment rather than choosing an isolator from a catalog based on a single number. Weight, load distribution, operating speed, excitation frequency, stiffness, damping, natural frequency, structural interfaces, environmental exposure, and allowable movement all influence the behavior of an isolation system.

That is why custom isolator design is best understood as an application-specific engineering process. The central relationship is straightforward but technically significant:

Equipment → operating loads → excitation → isolator properties → dynamic response → structural support

When seismic requirements apply, the system expands to include movement control and anchorage:

Equipment → vibration isolator → seismic restraint → support → connection → anchorage → structure

Spring, wire rope, rubber/metal, and elastomeric systems each offer different characteristics. The correct selection depends on the actual requirements of the equipment and facility. For HVAC systems, rotating machinery, industrial equipment, marine engines, healthcare equipment, and other specialized applications, customization may be warranted when standard products cannot adequately address loading, geometry, dynamic performance, environmental conditions, or seismic integration.

The engineering process should also continue beyond the isolator itself. Structural attachments, MEP coordination, BIM models, fabrication tolerances, mounting interfaces, and field installation can all affect the final result. Codes and project requirements such as IBC, ASCE 7, CBC, HCAI requirements, ACI 318, AISC standards, manufacturer criteria, project specifications, and AHJ requirements should be evaluated according to the specific application rather than treated as interchangeable requirements.

For The Sigma Source, this creates a practical connection between vibration control, seismic engineering, structural analysis, BIM/CAD coordination, and custom metal fabrication. The objective is not to make every isolation system custom. It is to determine when customization provides the engineering characteristics and integration required by the project, then carry that design through coordination and fabrication with the same technical discipline applied to the initial analysis.
