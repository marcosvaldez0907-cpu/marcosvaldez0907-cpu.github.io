Vibration Isolation: Systems, Isolators, Design & Applications

anitaa September 28, 2026

Vibration isolation is an engineered approach to reducing the transmission of mechanical vibration from equipment into the supporting structure or from surrounding structures into sensitive equipment. In commercial buildings, industrial facilities, healthcare environments, laboratories, and specialized equipment installations, the objective is not simply to place a resilient material beneath a machine. Effective vibration isolation requires the relationship between equipment mass, operating speed, excitation frequency, isolator stiffness, damping, structural support, and connected services to be understood as one dynamic system.

A properly selected vibration isolation system can help reduce structure-borne vibration generated by pumps, fans, compressors, chillers, air-handling equipment, motors, generators, manufacturing machinery, and other rotating or reciprocating equipment. Depending on the application, engineers may specify spring vibration isolators, elastomeric or rubber/metal mounts, wire rope isolators, acoustic isolation hangers, floor-mounted isolation systems, or captive configurations.

The engineering challenge is determining which isolation method is appropriate for the equipment and its operating environment. A mount with adequate load capacity may still provide poor isolation if its stiffness, natural frequency, or damping characteristics are inappropriate for the excitation frequency. Likewise, an isolation system can perform differently from its intended design if piping, ductwork, conduit, structural connections, or seismic restraints create unintended vibration transmission paths.

For engineers, contractors, architects, facility managers, and specification writers, vibration isolation therefore needs to be treated as a system-level design consideration. The technical brief for this topic identifies equipment loading, center of gravity, operating speed, excitation frequency, structural support, environmental conditions, and installation constraints as important factors in custom isolation design.

What Is Vibration Isolation?

Vibration isolation is the use of a resilient mechanical interface to reduce the transmission of vibratory forces or motion between a vibration source and another system. In a typical equipment installation, the basic path can be represented as:

Equipment → Isolator → Support structure

Without an isolation element, vibration generated by rotating machinery can pass directly into a concrete housekeeping pad, structural floor, steel frame, roof structure, or other supporting assembly. The resulting structure-borne vibration may then travel through the building and become noticeable at locations far from the original equipment.

A vibration isolator changes this transmission path by introducing controlled compliance between the equipment and its support. Springs, elastomers, wire rope, and other resilient elements can provide the required stiffness characteristics while allowing the equipment to operate without being rigidly coupled to the structure.

The performance of an isolation system depends on more than material selection. Engineers consider the equipment's operating frequency, system natural frequency, static deflection, damping, dynamic stiffness, equipment weight, mounting geometry, and expected movement. These parameters determine whether the system can reduce transmitted vibration under actual operating conditions.

Vibration isolation should also be distinguished from vibration damping. Isolation focuses on reducing the transmission of vibration between systems. Damping dissipates vibratory energy and can influence resonance and dynamic response. An isolation system can incorporate damping, but damping alone does not necessarily provide effective isolation.

For example, a high-speed pump installed on an improperly selected resilient mount may continue transmitting substantial vibration if the isolation system operates too close to a resonant condition. Conversely, an appropriately engineered system can place the equipment-support relationship in a more favorable dynamic range.

The practical objective is not necessarily to eliminate all movement. Some movement can be expected from a compliant isolation system. The engineering goal is to achieve the required reduction in transmitted vibration while maintaining equipment stability, appropriate clearances, acceptable displacement, and compatibility with the surrounding mechanical and structural systems.

Why Vibration Isolation Matters in Buildings and Industrial Facilities

Mechanical equipment is a common source of vibration in modern buildings. Fans, pumps, compressors, chillers, air-handling units, cooling equipment, generators, and motors all generate dynamic forces during operation. If those forces are transferred directly into a structure, they can create vibration that affects equipment, occupants, building components, or sensitive operations.

One important concern is equipment performance. Excessive vibration can contribute to mechanical wear, bearing loads, fatigue, fastener problems, misalignment, and reduced service life. The consequences depend on the equipment and operating environment, so vibration isolation should be coordinated with the manufacturer's requirements and the project's performance criteria.

Structure-borne vibration is another concern. A vibration source located in a mechanical room can transmit energy through a concrete slab or structural frame into adjacent spaces. In healthcare facilities, laboratories, offices, residential areas, and other sensitive environments, this transmission can become an operational or occupant-comfort issue.

Noise can also be related to vibration transmission. Structural vibration may excite floors, walls, ceilings, piping, and other building components, creating secondary sound. Vibration isolation can reduce one pathway for noise transmission, but it should not be treated as a universal acoustic solution. Airborne sound, equipment-generated noise, ductborne transmission, and architectural acoustics may require separate treatment.

The importance of isolation becomes particularly apparent when equipment is installed near sensitive spaces. A hospital imaging area, research laboratory, precision manufacturing environment, or technical facility may have vibration criteria that are substantially more restrictive than those for ordinary mechanical spaces.

Industrial applications can present additional challenges. Machinery may experience variable operating speeds, startup and shutdown conditions, shock loads, changing process loads, or harsh environmental exposure. Marine and mobile equipment can introduce additional axial, radial, and multi-axis dynamic loads.

For these reasons, vibration isolation should be considered during design rather than added only after excessive vibration appears. Early coordination allows engineers to consider equipment characteristics, support conditions, connected services, structural behavior, and maintenance requirements before installation details become difficult to change.

How Engineers Evaluate a Vibration Isolation Problem

Selecting an isolation system starts with understanding the equipment and the environment in which it will operate. Nominal equipment weight alone is rarely sufficient for a detailed isolation selection.

Equipment Weight and Load Distribution

The operating weight of the equipment determines the load carried by the isolation system. Engineers must also understand how that load is distributed among individual mounting points. Center of gravity, mounting geometry, equipment frame stiffness, and the number and location of isolators can all influence the actual load at each support.

Uneven loading can produce different deflections between mounts and may affect equipment stability. A design should therefore consider the load carried by each isolator rather than assuming that total equipment weight can always be divided equally.

Operating Speed and Excitation Frequency

Rotating equipment generates excitation related to operating speed and machine characteristics. RPM can be converted to rotational frequency, while additional excitation may occur at harmonics or from other mechanical phenomena.

The relationship between excitation frequency and the natural frequency of the isolation system is fundamental. An isolator that appears appropriate based solely on static load capacity may not provide the desired dynamic performance.

Structural Support Conditions

The same equipment can behave differently depending on whether it is installed on a concrete slab, housekeeping pad, structural steel frame, suspended support, equipment base, or specialized foundation.

The supporting structure can influence dynamic response. Engineers should also consider whether the structure itself has vibration-sensitive characteristics or whether vibration can travel through connected components.

Environmental Conditions

Temperature, moisture, chemicals, oil, diesel fuel, outdoor exposure, corrosion, and marine environments can affect material selection and long-term performance. Elastomeric materials require consideration of their operating environment, while metal components may require appropriate corrosion protection.

The technical brief identifies environmental exposure, maintenance access, corrosion, installation limitations, dynamic loads, and structural support conditions among the practical concerns that technical buyers may face.

This engineering assessment establishes the information needed before selecting the isolator type, capacity, stiffness, configuration, and associated hardware.

Key Engineering Principles Behind Vibration Isolation

The effectiveness of a vibration isolation system depends on several related dynamic parameters. Understanding these principles helps engineers evaluate whether an isolation system is appropriate for a specific application.

Natural Frequency

Natural frequency describes the frequency at which a dynamic system tends to oscillate when disturbed. For an isolation system, natural frequency is strongly influenced by stiffness and supported mass.

A basic conceptual relationship is that increasing compliance generally lowers the natural frequency, while increasing stiffness raises it. The actual system behavior can be more complex because equipment, bases, structural supports, and multiple isolators can interact dynamically.

Static Deflection

Static deflection is the displacement produced by the supported load under static conditions. For spring systems, static deflection is closely related to stiffness and natural frequency.

Greater static deflection generally corresponds to a more compliant spring system and can support lower natural frequencies. However, increasing deflection is not automatically better. Equipment stability, available clearance, operating displacement, seismic requirements, and installation constraints must also be considered.

Transmissibility

Transmissibility describes how vibration or force is transferred through a dynamic system. The relationship between excitation frequency and isolation-system natural frequency strongly affects transmissibility.

Isolation generally becomes more effective when the excitation frequency is sufficiently separated from the system's natural frequency. Operation near resonance can instead produce amplified motion, making proper engineering and selection particularly important.

Damping

Damping controls how energy is dissipated within a dynamic system. It can reduce resonant amplification and influence transient response.

However, damping and isolation involve tradeoffs. Increasing damping can reduce resonance response, while excessive damping can influence isolation performance in the operating frequency range. The appropriate characteristics depend on the application.

Dynamic Stiffness

Dynamic stiffness describes how an isolation material or assembly responds under dynamic loading. For elastomeric systems, dynamic properties can differ from simple static stiffness because material behavior depends on frequency, temperature, strain amplitude, and other factors.

These relationships demonstrate why vibration isolation is an engineering problem rather than simply a product-selection exercise. Static load capacity, material type, and physical dimensions are important, but they must be evaluated alongside dynamic requirements.

Types of Vibration Isolation Systems

Different equipment and installation conditions call for different vibration isolation technologies. No single isolator type is appropriate for every application.

Spring Vibration Isolators

Spring isolators are widely used for mechanical equipment where substantial static deflection and relatively low natural frequencies may be required. They can be applied to equipment such as air-handling units, pumps, fans, chillers, and other mechanical systems.

Restrained or captive spring configurations may be appropriate when controlled movement or mechanical retention is important. The selection depends on equipment loading, movement requirements, seismic design, and installation geometry.

Rubber and Elastomeric Vibration Isolators

Rubber and elastomeric mounts provide resilient support in compact configurations. They can be useful where space limitations, moderate loads, or specific damping characteristics make elastomeric isolation appropriate.

Material selection should consider temperature, environmental exposure, chemical compatibility, aging, and expected dynamic loading. Different elastomer formulations do not behave identically, so material selection should correspond to the actual application.

Wire Rope Vibration Isolators

Wire rope isolators use formed metal cable to provide resilient support. They can offer useful characteristics for applications involving multi-axis vibration, shock, and demanding environmental conditions.

They are frequently considered for specialized industrial, marine, aerospace, and equipment applications where traditional rubber or spring configurations may not provide the desired combination of movement control, environmental resistance, and dynamic behavior.

Acoustic Isolation Hangers

Acoustic isolation hangers are commonly used for suspended mechanical systems. HVAC equipment and associated services can transmit vibration through hanger rods and structural connections, making the suspended support itself part of the isolation path.

Floor Vibration Isolators

Floor-mounted isolation systems provide a resilient interface between equipment and a structural floor or equipment base. Depending on the equipment, these systems may be combined with inertia bases, rails, brackets, or other support assemblies.

Captive Vibration Isolators

Captive configurations provide controlled mechanical retention while maintaining resilient isolation. They can be useful where equipment movement must be limited or where the mounting assembly must remain mechanically integrated under specific operating conditions.

How to Choose the Right Vibration Isolator

Isolator selection should begin with the equipment's dynamic requirements rather than with a preferred product category. The correct system must support the equipment while achieving the required isolation performance and remaining compatible with the installation.

First, determine the operating weight and load carried by each isolator. Consider the equipment's center of gravity and mounting-point geometry because these factors can create unequal loads.

Next, identify operating speed and excitation frequencies. A fan operating at a particular RPM may have a different isolation requirement from a compressor or reciprocating machine even when their weights are similar.

Natural frequency is another central selection criterion. The isolation system should be evaluated in relation to the equipment's excitation frequencies so that the intended operating range is separated appropriately from resonance.

Equipment movement must also be considered. Some installations can tolerate greater vertical or lateral displacement, while others require tighter movement control. Startup and shutdown conditions may produce transient behavior that differs from steady-state operation.

The installation environment also affects the decision. Indoor HVAC equipment, outdoor mechanical equipment, marine engines, industrial machinery, and chemically exposed installations can have substantially different material and corrosion-protection requirements.

Maintenance is another practical factor. Isolation components should be accessible for inspection where required, and the design should consider long-term service conditions and replacement constraints.

For custom applications, the available information should include equipment dimensions, operating weight, mounting locations, center of gravity, RPM, excitation characteristics, structural support, environmental exposure, required movement limits, seismic requirements, and project specifications. The brief specifically identifies these application-specific variables as central to custom isolation design.

Vibration Isolation for HVAC and MEP Equipment

HVAC and MEP systems are among the most common applications for vibration isolation because mechanical equipment can generate continuous dynamic forces and provide multiple paths for vibration transmission.

Air-handling units may be installed on spring isolators, elastomeric supports, or other isolation assemblies depending on their size, configuration, location, and performance requirements. Fans and pumps similarly require consideration of operating speed, equipment weight, mounting geometry, and support conditions.

Chillers and compressors can generate substantial dynamic forces and may require engineered isolation systems that address equipment loading, foundation conditions, piping connections, and service access. Rooftop installations introduce additional structural and environmental considerations.

Suspended mechanical equipment creates a different isolation path. Instead of transmitting vibration through floor-mounted supports, vibration can travel through hanger rods, structural attachments, duct connections, and piping. Acoustic isolation hangers and other resilient components can therefore become important parts of the overall system.

Piping and ductwork deserve particular attention. An isolated piece of equipment can still transmit vibration through a rigid pipe or duct connection that bypasses the intended isolation path. Flexible connectors may be required where appropriate, but their selection and installation must be coordinated with pressure, movement, thermal, seismic, and operational requirements.

Electrical conduit and cable connections can also create unintended paths depending on the equipment configuration.

For this reason, HVAC vibration isolation should not be evaluated only by asking which mount goes underneath the equipment. The equipment, isolators, base, structure, piping, ductwork, electrical connections, and restraints should be considered as an interconnected system.

The goal is to achieve the required reduction in transmitted vibration without compromising mechanical functionality, structural performance, seismic requirements, or serviceability.

Vibration Isolation for Rotating and Industrial Equipment

Industrial equipment presents a broad range of vibration-control challenges. Motors, pumps, compressors, fans, generators, engines, machine tools, and manufacturing equipment can operate at different speeds and produce different excitation characteristics.

Rotating equipment can generate vibration through imbalance, misalignment, bearing conditions, gear interactions, or other mechanical sources. Isolation can reduce the transmission of these forces to the supporting structure, but it does not replace proper equipment alignment, balancing, maintenance, or mechanical troubleshooting.

Generators and engines may require isolation systems that accommodate significant dynamic loading while maintaining controlled movement. Depending on the application, spring, elastomeric, wire-rope, or captive systems may be considered.

Manufacturing equipment can also require specialized isolation where vibration from one machine could affect another machine or sensitive production process. The support structure, neighboring equipment, operating frequencies, and process requirements can all influence the design.

Marine applications introduce additional factors. Engines and propulsion systems may experience shock, vibration, multi-axis movement, moisture, salt exposure, and changing operating conditions. Metal wire-rope systems and specialized engine mounts may be considered where their characteristics match the application.

The Sigma Source's technical brief specifically identifies marine and mobile equipment as applications in which shock loads, axial and radial loads, environmental exposure, and controlled movement can affect isolator selection.

Industrial vibration isolation should therefore begin with a clear understanding of the equipment's operating envelope. A solution that performs adequately at steady-state speed may behave differently during startup, shutdown, changing loads, or abnormal operating conditions.

For procurement teams, the specification should communicate these requirements clearly. Engineers and manufacturers need sufficient information to evaluate capacity, stiffness, dynamic characteristics, material compatibility, mounting geometry, and any required restraints or fabricated supports.

Vibration Isolation in Healthcare, Laboratories, and Sensitive Facilities

Healthcare and laboratory environments can impose more demanding vibration-control requirements because mechanical vibration may affect sensitive equipment, imaging systems, research instruments, measurements, or occupant conditions.

Hospital mechanical systems may include chillers, pumps, fans, air-handling units, compressors, and other equipment located near patient-care areas or technically sensitive spaces. Isolation design must account for the equipment's dynamic characteristics while also coordinating with the building's structural and mechanical systems.

Medical imaging and diagnostic equipment can be particularly sensitive to vibration depending on the technology and manufacturer requirements. In these installations, vibration criteria should come from the applicable equipment documentation, project specifications, and facility requirements rather than from a generic isolation assumption.

Laboratories can present similar challenges. Sensitive instrumentation may be affected by vibration generated by nearby mechanical equipment, building occupants, or other machinery. The solution may require consideration of both the source and the receiving equipment.

Structural transmission paths become especially important in sensitive facilities. Vibration can travel through floors, columns, framing, walls, piping, ducts, and other connected elements. Isolating one piece of equipment may not resolve a problem if another vibration source remains coupled to the same structural system.

Healthcare projects in California may also involve HCAI requirements and project-specific criteria. Building-code and seismic requirements should be coordinated with the vibration-control design rather than treated as interchangeable standards.

For facility managers, the practical objective is often long-term performance. Isolation systems should be accessible, compatible with maintenance procedures, and appropriate for the environmental conditions of the mechanical space.

The technical brief identifies hospitals, laboratories, sensitive equipment, and high-performance facilities as important applications where vibration criteria may be more restrictive.

Vibration Isolation and Seismic Restraint

Vibration isolation and seismic restraint address different dynamic conditions and should not be treated as synonymous.

Vibration isolation reduces normal operational vibration transmission.

Seismic restraint limits or controls equipment movement associated with earthquake loading.

An isolated piece of mechanical equipment may therefore require additional provisions for seismic conditions depending on the building, equipment, location, applicable codes, project specifications, and engineering design criteria.

The challenge is that a vibration isolator is intentionally compliant, while seismic restraint may need to limit movement under earthquake loading. The two requirements must be coordinated so that the restraint system does not unintentionally create a rigid vibration-transmission path during normal equipment operation.

Restrained spring isolators, seismic snubbers, restraint brackets, anchors, and other hardware can be incorporated into an overall system when required. Their configuration, clearance, attachment, and capacity must correspond to the project design.

For California projects, the International Building Code as adopted and modified through the California Building Code, along with applicable project requirements and seismic design provisions, can influence the design of nonstructural mechanical systems. ASCE 7 provides important seismic design provisions, but it should not be described as a standalone vibration-isolation standard. The brief specifically emphasizes this distinction.

Healthcare facilities may introduce additional HCAI requirements and project-specific criteria.

This coordination is particularly important for engineers and contractors because an isolation system can perform well dynamically yet become ineffective if restraints, piping, ductwork, or structural connections bypass the intended isolation path.

A successful design therefore considers normal operation and seismic conditions together while preserving the required functionality of both systems.

Codes, Standards, and Project Requirements for Vibration Isolation

Vibration isolation projects in the United States are governed by a combination of building codes, structural requirements, equipment specifications, project criteria, and application-specific engineering requirements.

ASCE 7 is particularly relevant to seismic design, including provisions affecting nonstructural components and their attachments. Its role should be understood within the broader structural and seismic design framework rather than presented as a general vibration-isolation performance specification.

The International Building Code provides a broader building-code framework, while the California Building Code incorporates California-specific requirements. Projects in California may therefore require coordination between the applicable CBC provisions, structural design criteria, mechanical requirements, and project specifications.

Healthcare projects can involve the California Department of Health Care Access and Information, or HCAI, formerly associated with OSHPD terminology. Where HCAI requirements apply, engineers and contractors must coordinate the isolation and restraint design with the applicable healthcare facility requirements and project documentation.

Code compliance is only one part of the design process. Equipment manufacturer requirements, mechanical specifications, structural support conditions, seismic criteria, vibration criteria, and installation constraints can all affect the final isolation system.

For example, an isolator may have sufficient vertical capacity but still be inappropriate because its movement characteristics conflict with connected piping or because the equipment requires a different dynamic response. Similarly, a system may satisfy a structural attachment requirement while failing to provide the vibration performance expected by the equipment specification.

This is why vibration isolation should be documented through coordinated engineering rather than reduced to a catalog selection.

Custom Vibration Isolation Engineering

Standard isolation products can address many common applications, but some installations require a customized engineering approach. Customization may be necessary when equipment has unusual dimensions, nonuniform loading, specialized operating frequencies, limited mounting locations, severe environmental exposure, or restricted installation space.

A custom vibration isolation system may include fabricated equipment bases, isolation rails, mounting brackets, structural frames, custom spring assemblies, specialized wire-rope configurations, or integrated restraint hardware.

Engineering begins with application data. Equipment weight, center of gravity, mounting points, operating speed, excitation frequency, dynamic loads, structural support, environmental conditions, movement limits, and seismic requirements all influence the design.

BIM and 3D CAD modeling can support this process by allowing isolation assemblies to be coordinated with equipment, structure, piping, ductwork, electrical systems, and architectural constraints before fabrication. Detailed models and fabrication drawings can help identify interference conditions that might otherwise be discovered during installation.

Custom metal fabrication can also be integrated into the isolation solution. Stainless steel, carbon steel, aluminum, structural steel, and sheet metal can be selected according to structural, environmental, and fabrication requirements. Processing may include laser cutting, plasma cutting, welding, forming, machining, galvanizing, or powder coating.

This combination of engineering and fabrication is particularly valuable when a project requires a complete assembly rather than an isolated component.

The Sigma Source's technical brief identifies custom isolation as an opportunity to connect application-specific design, structural analysis, BIM/CAD coordination, and fabrication.

The resulting system should be treated as an engineered assembly whose components work together to satisfy dynamic, structural, installation, environmental, and project-specific requirements.

Vibration Isolation Design and Installation Considerations

Even a properly selected vibration isolator can underperform if the installation does not preserve the intended isolation path. Equipment alignment, load distribution, clearances, connected services, and restraints all influence real-world behavior.

Equipment should be positioned so that loads are distributed appropriately across the isolation points. Improper alignment can create unequal loading and unwanted movement.

Connected piping and ductwork should be evaluated carefully. Rigid connections can bypass isolation and transfer vibration directly into the building. Flexible connections may help maintain separation, but they must be selected and installed for the actual pressure, temperature, movement, and seismic conditions.

Clearance is also important. A restrained system requires enough movement space to operate without unintended contact, while seismic restraints may require specific clearances to remain inactive during normal operation and engage when required.

Installation should also account for equipment access, inspection, maintenance, and replacement. Environmental exposure can affect the long-term performance of elastomers, springs, wire rope, coatings, and fabricated components.

For retrofit projects, existing structural conditions may impose additional limitations. Engineers may need to work around existing housekeeping pads, steel framing, piping, ductwork, electrical systems, and equipment access routes.

The central principle is that isolation performance belongs to the complete installation, not only to the isolator itself. The technical brief specifically notes that the article should avoid implying that selecting the correct isolator alone guarantees system performance.

For complex projects, coordination among the equipment manufacturer, structural engineer, MEP engineer, contractor, fabricator, and installation team can significantly reduce the risk of unintended vibration paths or installation conflicts.

Conclusion: Designing Vibration Isolation as an Integrated Engineering System

Effective vibration isolation begins with a clear understanding of the vibration source, the equipment's operating characteristics, and the structure or system receiving the transmitted forces. Spring isolators, elastomeric mounts, wire rope isolators, acoustic hangers, floor isolation systems, and captive configurations each provide different mechanical characteristics. The appropriate choice depends on the application rather than on a universal hierarchy of isolator types.

The most important design variables include equipment weight, individual isolator loading, center of gravity, operating speed, excitation frequency, natural frequency, static deflection, damping, dynamic stiffness, expected movement, structural support, environmental exposure, and connected services. These factors determine whether an isolation system can achieve the intended reduction in transmitted vibration while maintaining equipment stability and operational requirements.

The broader installation must also be considered. Piping, ductwork, conduit, structural connections, equipment bases, and restraints can create alternate transmission paths. Seismic restraint introduces another layer of design because vibration isolation and seismic protection address different dynamic conditions and must be coordinated rather than treated as interchangeable functions.

For U.S. construction projects, the design should also account for applicable building codes, seismic requirements, project specifications, equipment manufacturer requirements, and healthcare-specific criteria where applicable. ASCE 7, IBC, CBC, and HCAI requirements can form part of this framework, but none should be treated as a substitute for application-specific vibration engineering.

For complex commercial, industrial, healthcare, marine, and infrastructure applications, custom engineering may be necessary when standard isolation products cannot adequately address equipment geometry, dynamic characteristics, environmental conditions, structural constraints, or seismic requirements.

Frequently Asked Questions About Vibration Isolation

What is vibration isolation?

Vibration isolation is an engineering method for reducing the transmission of vibration between a source and another system. In mechanical equipment applications, an isolator is placed between equipment and its support to introduce controlled compliance into the transmission path.

The isolation system changes how dynamic forces are transferred into the supporting structure. Depending on the application, this may involve springs, elastomers, wire rope, resilient hangers, or other isolation components. Effective performance depends on the relationship between equipment mass, stiffness, natural frequency, damping, excitation frequency, and installation conditions.

How does a vibration isolator work?

A vibration isolator works by introducing a resilient mechanical interface between equipment and its support. The isolator has stiffness and damping characteristics that influence the dynamic response of the equipment-support system.

The relationship between the equipment's excitation frequency and the isolation system's natural frequency is particularly important. When the system is appropriately designed, the isolation interface can reduce the amount of vibratory force transmitted into the supporting structure.

The isolator must still support the equipment safely and maintain acceptable movement. Consequently, load capacity alone is not enough to establish whether an isolator is suitable.

What is the difference between vibration isolation and vibration damping?

Vibration isolation and damping address different aspects of dynamic behavior.

Isolation is primarily concerned with reducing transmission between a source and another system. Damping dissipates vibratory energy and influences the magnitude and duration of dynamic response, particularly near resonance.

An isolation system may include damping characteristics, but damping does not automatically produce effective isolation. Engineers should evaluate stiffness, natural frequency, excitation frequency, transmissibility, and damping together when designing a system.

How do engineers choose the right vibration isolation mount?

Selection typically begins with equipment weight and individual mounting-point loads. Engineers then consider center of gravity, mounting geometry, operating RPM, excitation frequencies, desired natural frequency, movement requirements, structural support, environmental exposure, and project-specific criteria.

Spring, elastomeric, wire-rope, floor-mounted, acoustic-hanger, and captive systems each have different characteristics. The appropriate selection depends on the actual application.

For complex installations, engineers may also need equipment drawings, operating data, structural information, environmental conditions, seismic requirements, and details of connected piping and ductwork.

Are spring isolators better than rubber vibration isolators?

There is no universal answer because spring and elastomeric isolators are engineered for different performance characteristics.

Spring systems can provide substantial static deflection and may be suitable where relatively low natural frequencies are required. Elastomeric mounts can provide compact resilient support and may be advantageous where space, damping, environmental conditions, or load characteristics favor that approach.

The comparison should therefore be based on the equipment and application rather than a general claim that one technology is inherently superior.

What is static deflection in a vibration isolation system?

Static deflection is the displacement of an isolator under the supported static load. It is related to isolator stiffness and, particularly for spring systems, has an important relationship with natural frequency.

Static deflection is one of several parameters used to evaluate an isolation system. Engineers must also consider dynamic loading, excitation frequency, damping, movement, equipment stability, seismic requirements, and installation clearances.

Can vibration isolation systems also provide seismic protection?

An isolation system can be configured with seismic restraint provisions, but vibration isolation and seismic restraint perform different functions.

Vibration isolation addresses normal operating vibration transmission. Seismic restraint addresses movement associated with earthquake loading. Depending on the project, an isolated system may require restrained springs, snubbers, anchors, brackets, or other seismic provisions.

The applicable requirements depend on the equipment, building, location, structural design, project specifications, and applicable codes. The two functions should be coordinated so seismic restraints do not unintentionally create unwanted vibration paths during normal operation.

What vibration isolation is used for HVAC equipment?

HVAC applications can use spring isolators, elastomeric mounts, acoustic isolation hangers, floor isolation systems, restrained configurations, and other engineered assemblies.

The appropriate choice depends on equipment type, operating speed, equipment weight, mounting arrangement, structural support, required isolation performance, movement, and seismic conditions.

Air-handling units, fans, pumps, chillers, compressors, cooling equipment, and suspended mechanical systems can have substantially different isolation requirements.

When should a custom vibration isolator be used?

Custom vibration isolation may be appropriate when standard products cannot adequately address unusual equipment geometry, load distribution, operating frequency, environmental exposure, movement requirements, space restrictions, or retrofit conditions.

Custom solutions can include fabricated bases, brackets, rails, frames, mounting assemblies, and integrated isolation and restraint components.

The design should begin with application data rather than fabrication alone. Equipment weight, center of gravity, mounting locations, operating speed, excitation characteristics, support conditions, environmental exposure, and project requirements are important inputs.

Does vibration isolation eliminate noise?

Vibration isolation can reduce structure-borne vibration and may reduce noise associated with vibration transmission, but it does not automatically eliminate all noise.

Airborne noise, ductborne noise, equipment-generated sound, architectural transmission, and other acoustic paths may require separate treatment. A complete acoustic strategy may therefore combine vibration isolation with appropriate acoustic design.

What information is needed to design a vibration isolation system?

Useful design information includes equipment operating weight, dimensions, center of gravity, mounting-point locations, operating RPM, excitation frequencies, expected dynamic loads, structural support conditions, environmental exposure, required movement limits, seismic requirements, and project specifications.

Information about connected piping, ductwork, electrical systems, flexible connections, and restraints may also be necessary because these components can affect the overall isolation path.

For custom systems, detailed equipment drawings and installation constraints can help engineers coordinate the isolation assembly with the structure and surrounding MEP systems.

Does vibration isolation require seismic restraints in California?

There is no single answer that applies to every installation. Requirements depend on the applicable building-code provisions, seismic design criteria, equipment characteristics, project specifications, building location, support configuration, and installation conditions.

California projects may involve the California Building Code and applicable seismic provisions, while healthcare facilities may also involve HCAI requirements. ASCE 7 can form part of the seismic design framework.

The vibration isolation system and seismic restraint should therefore be evaluated together by the responsible design professionals rather than assuming that every isolated equipment installation requires the same restraint configuration.
