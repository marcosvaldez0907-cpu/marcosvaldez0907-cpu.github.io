What Does a Seismic Structural Engineer Do?

A seismic structural engineer evaluates how a building or structural component is expected to respond to earthquake-induced forces and determines whether the structure, its members, and relevant connections have adequate capacity for the applicable design conditions. The work is not limited to calculating a single seismic force. It involves understanding the structural system, load path, material properties, geometry, connections, foundation conditions, and interaction between structural and nonstructural components.

For a new building, a structural engineer for seismic design may participate in selecting or evaluating the lateral force-resisting system, including moment frames, braced frames, shear walls, and diaphragms. The engineer considers how seismic forces are transferred through floors and roofs into vertical resisting elements and ultimately into the foundation. Member forces, drift, stability, connections, anchorage, and applicable load combinations become part of the engineering analysis.

Existing buildings require a different process. A seismic structural assessment may begin with available drawings, previous calculations, field observations, and verification of existing conditions. The engineer may investigate structural framing, connections, foundations, alterations, deterioration, additions, and changes in occupancy or equipment loading. If deficiencies are identified, the next stage may involve seismic strengthening or retrofit design.

Seismic engineering can also address individual structural components. A rooftop air-handling unit, equipment platform, pipe support, steel bracket, concrete anchor, or structural attachment may introduce concentrated loads that require evaluation. In these cases, the engineer must understand both the supported component and the building structure receiving the load.

The result may include engineering calculations, structural drawings, connection details, retrofit details, BIM models, specifications, or construction support. The appropriate deliverables depend on the project scope and the requirements of the Engineer of Record, owner, contractor, and Authority Having Jurisdiction.

When Does a Project Need Seismic Structural Engineering?

A project may require seismic structural engineering services whenever earthquake-related demands affect the design, modification, evaluation, or support of a structure. New construction is an obvious example, but many engineering requests arise from changes to existing buildings. Installing heavy equipment, modifying structural framing, adding a rooftop unit, creating a new opening, expanding an industrial facility, or renovating a healthcare space can alter the structural load path and trigger the need for engineering review.

Existing-building work is particularly dependent on project-specific conditions. An owner may know that a building was constructed decades ago but have incomplete structural drawings or uncertain information about previous modifications. A seismic engineering consultant can help establish the existing structural condition before a retrofit or modification is designed. Field verification may be necessary to confirm member sizes, connection details, slab thicknesses, anchor conditions, or the actual configuration of structural framing.

Equipment projects can also create seismic engineering requirements. Chillers, pumps, air-handling units, cooling equipment, generators, electrical equipment, and other heavy systems can impose vertical and lateral demands on floors, roofs, support frames, or foundations. Their support system must be evaluated in relation to the structure rather than treated as an isolated piece of equipment.

Industrial facilities can present additional challenges because structural systems may support process equipment, piping, platforms, conveyors, tanks, or specialized machinery. In healthcare facilities, seismic considerations can extend to equipment, MEP infrastructure, and other systems whose continued function may be important after an earthquake.

The need for engineering should therefore be based on the actual project conditions, applicable code requirements, structural system, equipment characteristics, and project specifications. Geographic location is relevant, but the phrase “near me” should never substitute for evaluating whether the engineer has the appropriate technical experience, professional credentials, project scope, and authorization to perform the required work.

How Is Seismic Structural Analysis Performed?

Seismic structural analysis begins with defining the design criteria and understanding how seismic forces can enter and travel through the structure. Depending on the project, the engineer may review building geometry, structural materials, occupancy, site conditions, structural system, mass distribution, diaphragm behavior, and applicable seismic parameters before selecting an appropriate analytical approach.

ASCE 7 is commonly used to establish seismic design criteria in U.S. projects, while the applicable building code establishes the broader regulatory framework. The analysis may involve equivalent lateral force procedures, modal response spectrum analysis, or other methods appropriate to the structure and project requirements. The selected procedure depends on characteristics such as building configuration, height, structural system, regularity, and applicable code provisions.

A key concept is the load path. Earthquake-induced forces must be transferred through structural diaphragms and vertical elements into the foundation. An engineer therefore examines more than individual beam or column strength. Connections, collectors, diaphragms, braces, shear walls, moment frames, anchorage, and foundation elements all contribute to the complete seismic-resisting system.

Drift is another important consideration. Excessive story or interstory movement can affect structural components, architectural elements, MEP systems, equipment, and connections. For projects involving sensitive equipment or tightly coordinated MEP infrastructure, structural movement may need to be considered alongside support and restraint requirements.

Material behavior also affects the analysis. Structural steel connections may require consideration of strength, ductility, bolt behavior, welding, and applicable AISC requirements. Reinforced-concrete elements require appropriate evaluation of reinforcement, concrete strength, anchorage, and applicable ACI provisions. The engineering process therefore connects seismic demand with the actual behavior and capacity of the structural system.

The final calculations should be sufficiently clear to demonstrate the assumptions, criteria, applied loads, analytical method, member checks, connection requirements, and conclusions relevant to the project.

What Are Included in Seismic Calculations for Buildings?

Seismic calculations for buildings are project-specific engineering documents rather than generic formulas that can be applied identically to every structure. Their scope depends on whether the work concerns new construction, an existing building, a structural modification, a retrofit, or a specific component such as equipment anchorage.

A calculation package may establish applicable seismic design parameters and load combinations before evaluating the structural system or component under consideration. For a building, this can include seismic weight, lateral forces, distribution of seismic demand, drift, structural member forces, stability, diaphragm actions, and reactions transferred to foundations. For a localized modification, the calculations may focus instead on new loads, connection forces, anchor capacity, supporting members, and load transfer into the existing structure.

Connection and anchorage calculations can be especially important. A structural bracket that appears physically substantial may still be inadequate if the supporting member, welds, bolts, gusset plates, or concrete anchors cannot transfer the required seismic demand. Similarly, an anchor may have sufficient nominal strength but still require evaluation for the actual concrete condition, edge distance, spacing, embedment, cracked concrete assumptions, or installation requirements applicable to the selected anchoring system.

For steel construction, AISC 360 and, where applicable, AISC 341 or AISC 358 may become relevant depending on the structural system and connection type. Concrete design and anchorage may involve ACI 318. Proprietary anchors may also require review of applicable ICC evaluation reports and manufacturer requirements.

For MEP or equipment support, calculations can connect equipment weight and seismic demand to the support assembly, structural attachment, and building structure. This is where structural engineering intersects with seismic bracing and equipment restraint.

Good engineering documentation makes these relationships traceable. The objective is not simply to produce a numerical result, but to demonstrate how the design criteria become forces, how those forces move through the assembly, and how each critical component and connection resists them.

How Are Existing Buildings Evaluated for Seismic Retrofit?

A seismic engineering for existing buildings project generally begins with understanding what is actually present. Existing-condition assessment can involve reviewing original drawings, previous renovation documents, structural calculations, site observations, photographs, material information, and available records. When documentation is incomplete, field verification becomes particularly important.

The engineer may investigate the condition and configuration of beams, columns, joists, slabs, diaphragms, shear walls, moment frames, braced frames, foundations, and structural connections. Deterioration, corrosion, cracking, unauthorized modifications, removed members, new openings, and previous equipment installations can affect the reliability of an analytical model.

The next step is determining the structural implications of the identified conditions. A seismic retrofit engineer may evaluate whether existing members and connections can resist the applicable demand and whether the building provides a continuous load path. Deficiencies can occur at the member level, but they can also occur at connections or interfaces between structural elements.

Retrofit solutions depend on the deficiency. Possible approaches can include adding or strengthening steel members, reinforcing concrete elements, improving connections, adding bracing, strengthening diaphragms, modifying anchorage, or developing localized structural support. A retrofit should be designed around the actual failure mechanism and load path rather than simply adding material.

Construction constraints are also significant. Existing buildings may remain occupied during construction, limiting access and sequencing. Healthcare facilities may impose particularly strict requirements for infection control, shutdowns, temporary conditions, and documentation. Industrial facilities can have operational constraints, process equipment, and restricted work zones.

For a successful retrofit, structural analysis, detailing, constructability, field verification, and coordination must remain connected. The objective is to develop a strengthening strategy that addresses the identified seismic demand while remaining compatible with the existing building and practical construction conditions.

How Do Structural Connections and Anchorage Affect Seismic Performance?

Structural members cannot perform as intended if their connections cannot transfer the forces generated during an earthquake. This makes structural connections and anchorage a central part of seismic structural design. A complete load path must extend from the component experiencing seismic demand through its connection and supporting member into the primary structural system and ultimately to the foundation.

Steel connections may involve high-strength bolts, welds, angles, plates, gussets, moment connections, brace connections, and reinforcing plates. The engineer must consider the force path through the complete connection rather than checking only the most visible component. A gusset plate, for example, transfers forces between structural members and must be compatible with the connected members, fasteners, welds, and expected deformation behavior.

Concrete anchorage presents a different set of considerations. Cast-in anchors, post-installed anchors, anchor bolts, and embedded plates may transfer seismic loads into reinforced concrete. Capacity can depend on concrete strength, embedment, edge distance, spacing, reinforcement, cracked or uncracked concrete assumptions, installation conditions, and the characteristics of the selected anchor system.

Equipment and MEP support systems create another important interface. A seismic brace can be adequately sized while the attachment to the building structure remains inadequate. Conversely, a strong structural attachment does not automatically make the supported assembly compliant if the brace, support frame, hanger, or equipment connection is deficient.

For this reason, seismic code compliance engineering should consider the complete assembly. Applicable requirements can come from the building code, ASCE 7, ACI 318, AISC standards, manufacturer requirements, project specifications, and AHJ requirements depending on the application.

This systems-based approach is especially valuable for retrofit and equipment projects because existing structural conditions may limit where new attachments can be installed. BIM coordination and field verification can help identify those constraints before fabrication and installation.

How Does MEP Seismic Support Connect With Structural Engineering?

MEP systems do not exist independently of the building structure. Mechanical piping, HVAC ductwork, electrical conduit, cable trays, suspended equipment, and other systems depend on supports and structural attachments that must accommodate the forces and movements applicable to the project. This is why seismic bracing and supports are closely connected to structural engineering.

A typical MEP seismic support load path can be described as:

MEP equipment or distribution system → support assembly → seismic restraint → connection → anchor → structural framing

Each interface matters. A pipe trapeze may use strut channels, threaded rods, clamps, and lateral or longitudinal braces, but those components ultimately transfer forces into a beam, slab, wall, or other structural element. The receiving structure must be capable of resisting the resulting load.

The same principle applies to HVAC duct systems, cable trays, electrical conduit, and mechanical equipment. Equipment supports may require structural frames or brackets, while vibration-isolated equipment can require a coordinated approach to both operational vibration control and seismic restraint. A spring isolator or rubber/metal isolator addresses a different engineering objective from a seismic restraint; combining those functions requires appropriate restraint and attachment design.

How Do BIM, Fabrication, and Construction Coordination Improve Seismic Engineering?

Engineering calculations establish structural requirements, but successful implementation also depends on translating those requirements into coordinated physical components. BIM and 3D CAD modeling can support this transition by representing structural framing, equipment, MEP systems, support assemblies, and connection locations in a coordinated digital environment.

For seismic projects, modeling can help identify whether a brace has a clear installation path, whether an anchor location conflicts with reinforcement or existing framing, whether equipment clearances are adequate, and whether a support assembly can be fabricated as detailed. These issues are particularly important in mechanical rooms, equipment rooms, congested ceilings, rooftops, hospitals, data centers, and industrial facilities.

Once the engineering geometry and requirements are established, custom fabrication can provide a controlled transition from design to physical assembly. Structural steel, carbon steel, stainless steel, aluminum where appropriate, steel plate, tubing, angles, channels, and custom strut components can be fabricated according to project requirements. Processes such as laser cutting, plasma cutting, welding, forming, stamping, machining, galvanizing, and powder coating may be selected according to material, geometry, corrosion exposure, finish requirements, and fabrication specifications.

This does not mean every seismic project requires custom fabrication. Standard components may be entirely appropriate where their configuration, capacity, installation requirements, and project documentation satisfy the design. Custom fabrication becomes valuable when standard components cannot efficiently accommodate unusual geometry, concentrated loads, equipment configurations, or existing structural conditions.

Construction coordination closes the loop. Field verification can confirm existing conditions, while shop drawings and coordinated models can communicate the intended installation. Construction-stage questions may require engineering review when field conditions differ from the original assumptions.

The result is an engineering process that connects analysis to detailing, fabrication, installation, and verification rather than treating calculations as an isolated deliverable.

Which Codes and Standards Apply to Seismic Structural Engineering?

Seismic structural design in the United States is governed by the applicable building code, referenced standards, project specifications, and requirements imposed by the relevant Authority Having Jurisdiction. The exact combination depends on the project location, building type, structural system, materials, and scope.

ASCE 7 is particularly important because it provides criteria and procedures for determining environmental loads, including seismic design provisions and requirements applicable to certain nonstructural components. It works within the broader building-code framework rather than replacing the building code itself.

The IBC establishes a broad model-code framework for structural and seismic design, while the CBC governs projects subject to California's adopted building requirements. California projects therefore require attention to the applicable edition and amendments rather than relying on a generic statement that a building is simply “code compliant.”

For concrete structures and anchorage, ACI 318 may apply. Structural steel projects may involve AISC 360, with AISC 341 relevant to applicable seismic structural steel systems and AISC 358 relevant to qualifying prequalified moment connections. Welding and fabrication may also require applicable AWS provisions.

Healthcare projects require additional care. HCAI requirements can apply to California healthcare construction, and OSHPD remains relevant as legacy terminology in discussions of California healthcare facilities and historical pre-approval documentation. Project-specific HCAI requirements should be evaluated rather than generalized to every healthcare project.

Proprietary anchors and fastening systems may have ICC evaluation reports and manufacturer-specific installation requirements that affect their use and capacity.

Ultimately, seismic code compliance engineering is project-specific. The engineer must identify the applicable code edition, seismic criteria, material standards, project specifications, manufacturer requirements, and AHJ requirements before determining the final design basis.

Frequently Asked Questions About Seismic Structural Engineering

What is a seismic structural engineer?

A seismic structural engineer specializes in evaluating and designing structural systems for earthquake-related demands. Depending on the project, the work can include seismic structural analysis, structural design, seismic calculations, existing-building assessment, retrofit engineering, connection design, anchorage, and structural modifications. The engineer considers the building's lateral force-resisting system, structural members, connections, foundations, and applicable design criteria.

When should I hire a seismic structural engineer?

Engineering may be appropriate for new construction, seismic retrofit, structural modifications, building additions, major renovations, rooftop equipment, heavy mechanical systems, or evaluation of an existing structure. The specific need depends on project conditions and applicable code requirements. Early engineering involvement can also help identify structural feasibility before architectural, MEP, fabrication, or construction decisions become difficult to change.

What is seismic structural analysis?

Seismic structural analysis evaluates how a structure responds to earthquake-induced demands. Depending on the project, the analysis can consider seismic design parameters, building mass, lateral force-resisting systems, diaphragms, structural members, connections, drift, stability, and foundation reactions. The selected analytical procedure should be appropriate to the structure and applicable code requirements.

What are seismic calculations for buildings used for?

They document the engineering basis for seismic design or evaluation. Calculations may address building-level lateral forces, member capacity, drift, connections, anchorage, equipment supports, or retrofit components. A localized equipment-support calculation, for example, may trace seismic demand from the equipment through its support frame and attachment into the existing structure.

Can a seismic structural engineer evaluate an existing building?

Yes. Existing-building engineering commonly begins with available drawings and records followed by field verification where necessary. The engineer can evaluate structural framing, connections, foundations, diaphragms, deterioration, previous modifications, and other relevant conditions. If deficiencies are identified, the project may proceed to seismic retrofit design or structural strengthening.

What is the difference between seismic bracing and structural engineering?

Seismic bracing restrains systems or components against applicable seismic movement and forces. Structural engineering evaluates the building or supporting structure and determines whether the complete load path can resist the resulting demand. For MEP equipment, for example, a seismic brace may transfer forces into a structural beam or slab, making the connection and supporting structure part of the engineering problem.

Does ASCE 7 apply to every seismic structural project?

ASCE 7 is widely used for U.S. structural loading criteria, including seismic provisions, but the applicable requirements depend on the project, code adoption, scope, and jurisdiction. It should not be treated as the only governing document. The applicable building code, material standards, project specifications, AHJ requirements, and other referenced standards must also be considered.

Are California seismic engineering requirements different?

California projects are subject to the applicable California-adopted building requirements, including the CBC and referenced standards. Project-specific conditions and local requirements also matter. Healthcare construction can involve additional HCAI requirements. Therefore, a California seismic structural engineer should establish the applicable code edition and project criteria rather than applying a generic seismic design assumption.

Can seismic engineering include MEP equipment and supports?

Yes. Structural engineering can intersect with seismic supports for mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems. Chillers, pumps, air-handling units, piping, ductwork, cable trays, conduit, and other systems may require seismic restraint and structural attachment. The engineering should account for the complete load path from the supported component through the restraint and connection into the building structure.

What information should I provide when requesting seismic engineering services?

Useful information can include the project address and jurisdiction, building type, project scope, architectural and structural drawings, available as-built documents, equipment information, weights and dimensions, MEP layouts, existing structural conditions, previous calculations, proposed modifications, and known permit or AHJ requirements. For existing buildings, photographs and field information can help identify conditions that may require verification.

Can BIM and custom fabrication be part of a seismic engineering project?

They can be when the project benefits from coordinated modeling and specialized support assemblies. BIM/3D CAD can help coordinate structural framing, MEP systems, equipment, supports, and connection locations. Custom fabrication can then produce brackets, support frames, strut assemblies, plates, or other components when standard products do not adequately address the required geometry or loading.

How does seismic retrofit engineering differ from new structural design?

New design starts with a defined structural concept and allows the engineer to coordinate the lateral system, members, connections, and foundation from the beginning. Retrofit engineering must work with an existing structure whose geometry, materials, connections, deterioration, and previous modifications may not be fully documented. Field verification and constructability therefore become particularly important, and strengthening must address the identified deficiency without creating unintended problems elsewhere in the load path.
